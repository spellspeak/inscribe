# Brackenhythe: world bible

Working draft 7. This is the document we hand to anyone writing for the town. If it is not in here, it is not true yet. Where something must reach the player, we say so. Where something is just colour, we say that too.

## The pitch in one breath

A river town that gets its light and its heat out of the ground. More than two hundred years ago the people of Brackenhythe found a rock in the cliff that glows warm for seven years and then goes grey, and they built everything on it: their money, their calendar, their church-that-is-not-a-church, their fights. This winter the rock has started going grey early. Everybody has a theory. One person has the truth.

The player arrives as an outsider hired by the town to find out what is wrong. The story is a slow one. It is about who you believe and what it costs them to tell you.

## Setting

Brackenhythe sits where the river Wend comes out of the hills and goes slack enough to land a barge. The town is about four thousand people, a working quay (the Hythe), a cliff behind it (the Scarp) with a mine under it (the Underfold), and a stepped street (the Stair) that joins the two. It feels late medieval: timber and stone, wool and leather, barges and carts, bronze fittings and iron tools. Nobody is a knight. There is no court. There is a Moot, a Reeve, a Bailiff, a guild that behaves like a bank, and a crowd of people who have to buy their winter from the cliff.

The feel we want: damp, lit from below, prosperous and nervous. Every window has a warm amber glow in it. Nobody wants to think about where it comes from.

There is no magic. We say this plainly to writers because the rock looks like magic. It is not. Hearthspar is a stone with a property, the way a lodestone pulls iron. Nobody casts anything. Nobody is blessed. Prayers at the Old Hythe are prayers, and nothing answers them.

## Hearthspar (this is the heart of the game, players must hear all of it)

Hearthspar is a pale amber rock cut from the Underfold. Fresh from the seam it is "live": it glows about as bright as a good lamp and gives off a steady warmth you can feel from across a room. It does this on its own, with nothing added, day and night, for about seven years. Then it fades over a few weeks to a dull grey and is "spent". Spent spar is called greyspar and is worth almost nothing. People spread it on paths.

Everything we would use electricity for, Brackenhythe uses spar for. A knuckle of live spar (a piece the size of a child's fist) lights a room. A hearthblock (a cut block the size of a loaf) warms a house through a winter, set into the wall where we would put a fireplace. Richer houses have a block in every room. Poorer houses share one between two families and argue about it.

Rules of spar the writers must keep straight:

- Live spar glows and warms on its own. It needs no fuel, no flame, no tending. *Players must hear this.*
- It lasts about seven years and then greys. Seven years is the number everyone quotes, the way we quote a warranty. *Players must hear this.*
- Live spar rings when you tap it with iron. Greyspar thuds. Cracked spar is thrown in the river. The Hands teach this as a rhyme, and the rhyme is to be said exactly: "Ring, it's live. Thud, it's dead. Crack, it's for the river bed." *Say it exactly.*
- Too much spar in a shut room gives you sparflush: headache, nosebleed, shaking hands. More than three knuckles in a closed bedroom is asking for it. *Players must hear this.*
- Spar must never go in a fire. It does not explode. It splits with a loud crack and throws hot splinters. Children are taught this before they are taught their letters. *Background colour, but it stops writers inventing a gunpowder.*
- Spar can be "banked", meaning dimmed, by wrapping it in wet leather or shutting it in a spar-box. A spar-box is a bronze box with a tight lid. Every house has one for the summer. *Background colour.*
- Only the Underfold Hands may cut spar, and only the Reckoners may stamp it. Stamped spar carries the Heron mark, a little heron pressed into a lead seal on the box. Unstamped spar is called "fiddled" spar and selling it is a crime. *Players must hear this.*

## History (dated by the Compact calendar, see below)

1. **C.0, Thawmonth.** The Compact of Brackenhythe is signed on the Hythe between the town and Warden Gilbrand of Ormsgate. The town is freed of the Warden's tolls in return for a tenth of all spar cut, the Spar Tenth. The year count begins here. The Compact's first line is the town's motto and is carved over the Moothall door: "What the Scarp gives, the Wend carries." *Say the motto exactly. The year count matters: this is Compact Year 212, written C.212.*
2. **C.14.** Hobb Stannock, a spar-cutter who could do sums, founds the Honourable Fellowship of Reckoners to keep the town's tally-sticks in one place. The Fellowship becomes the Tallyhouse. There has been a Stannock at its Long Table ever since. *Players must hear that the Reckoners are old and that a Stannock runs them.*
3. **C.61, Deepmonth.** The Sump Year. The lowest gallery of the Underfold, the Sump, floods in a single night. Forty-one Hands drown. The Sump is sealed. The number is said at every Dim Days vigil and the writers must not round it. *Say it exactly: forty-one.*
4. **C.102.** After two seasons of barge-raids from downriver, the Moot hangs the Chain across the Wend below the Hythe. The Wendwatch is founded to keep it. *Players must hear that the Chain exists and when it is up.*
5. **C.161.** The Grey Winter. A bad seam is cut and sold, and half the town's new spar greys inside a year. The Hands learn to grade by the ring of the stone. The grading rhyme dates from here. *Players must hear this, because this winter looks like that one and people say so.*
6. **C.170.** The Eighth-day Ordinance fixes Tallyday as every eighth day: market on the Hythe, the Moot sits, the Reckoners open their books. *Background colour, but the eight-day week comes from it.*
7. **C.188.** A rockfall in the Underfold kills three men. Cadoc Hargest, brother to one of them, begins preaching at the Old Hythe that the Scarp "sleeps lighter every year" and that the town has been lit too long. His followers are called Dimmers. *Players must hear what a Dimmer is.*
8. **C.199.** The Flush Year. A bad spring for sparflush. Ottilie Quade, a leech, opens the Leechhouse at the top of the Stair and takes the flushed in for two pips a night. Her son Anselm runs it now. *Background colour.*
9. **C.206.** Idonea Vask becomes Warden at Ormsgate. The following year she demands a fifth of all spar instead of a tenth. *Players must hear this.*
10. **C.209, Sparmonth.** The Vask Accord. The Moot wins the Tenth back. How it was paid for is not public. The town celebrates with the Great Lighting: every house puts its spar in the window for a night, and the Wend is said to have glowed amber to the Chain. *Say the name exactly: the Vask Accord of C.209. The Great Lighting is colour.*
11. **C.211, Deepmonth.** The Hands seal the Sump again, "for flooding". *Players must hear this. It is the first loose thread.*
12. **C.212, Thawmonth.** Now. The first hearthblocks sold last Sparmonth come back to the Dryhouse grey. The Reeve hires an outsider, the player, to find out why.

## The calendar (players must hear the shape of it)

The year has nine months of forty days, and five Dim Days at midwinter that belong to no month. That is 365 days, which is deliberate, but nobody in the town knows or cares. The months are Thawmonth, Sowmonth, Greenmonth, Longmonth, Haymonth, Cornmonth, Fallmonth, Sparmonth and Deepmonth. Sparmonth is when the Hands cut hardest, before the cold.

Each month has five weeks of eight days. The eighth day is Tallyday. On Tallyday the market is held on the Hythe, the Moot sits in the Moothall, the Reckoners open their books and debts fall due. People say "next Tallyday" the way we say "next week".

The Dim Days come between Deepmonth and Thawmonth. By custom every house banks its spar in a spar-box for the five days and lives by fire and tallow. No contract is signed on a Dim Day. The Dimmers keep a vigil at the Old Hythe and read the forty-one names. *Background colour, except the count of five days and the no-contracts rule, which players should hear.*

Years are counted from the Compact. This is C.212.

## Geography: the places

**The Hythe.** The working quay and market. Barges from Ormsgate tie up here. On Tallyday it is stalls from end to end. Everything that goes in or out of the town crosses the Hythe. *Players must hear this is the market.*

**The Heron and Hook.** The inn on the Hythe, kept by Maren Tolliver. Named for the river bird and for the boat-hooks the Wendwatch carry. Beer, bread, a bed, and every piece of news that comes up the river. If the player is anywhere by default, it is here.

**The Tallyhouse.** The Reckoners' hall at the top of the Hythe. A stone ground floor with a counting floor, a strongroom, and one wall hung with tally-sticks going back to C.14. Upstairs is the Long Table where the Master Reckoner sits. Osmund Stannock is there most days. The Tallyhouse bell rings at noon and at dusk.

**The Stair.** The stepped street from the Hythe up to the foot of the Scarp. Shops, workshops and most of the town's houses are on it or off it. The Leechhouse is at the top.

**The Leechhouse.** Anselm Quade's house and sick-rooms, top of the Stair. Two pips a night for the flushed.

**The Underfold.** The mine under the Scarp. Its gate is at the top of the Stair. Four galleries, counted downward: the Lip, the Shelf, the Throat and the Sump. The Sump has been sealed since Deepmonth C.211. Only Hands go in.

**The Dryhouse.** The Hands' lodge beside the Underfold gate, where spar is cut to size, graded by ring, boxed and sent down to the Tallyhouse to be stamped. Gudrun Thresk's chair is by the door.

**The Watchhouse.** The Wendwatch's hall and lock-up, on the riverside below the Hythe where the Chain is hung. Teodor Marrick sleeps there more than at home.

**The Chain.** An iron chain across the Wend, raised at dusk and dropped at dawn by a windlass in the Watchhouse. Nothing passes the Chain on the water between dusk and dawn, whoever is asking and whatever they pay. *Players must hear this rule.*

**The Old Hythe.** A ruined landing half a mile upriver, where the town was before the Compact. The Dimmers meet there. Wenna Hargest is there most evenings.

**The Moothall.** The town's hall on the Hythe, where the Moot of twelve sits on Tallyday and the Reeve keeps the Compact under glass. The motto is cut over the door.

**Ormsgate.** Off the map. The Warden's city, three days downriver by barge and six by road. Everything Brackenhythe does not make comes from there, and all the spar the town does not burn goes there.

## Factions

**The Honourable Fellowship of Reckoners, "the Reckoners".** Hobb Stannock's guild. They hold deposits in the strongroom, lend money at an increase, write notes that are good at any counting-house downriver, keep every debt in the town on a tally-stick, and stamp every box of spar with the Heron mark before it may be sold. They are the town's bank, its mint and its stamp in one, and they know it. Their Master is Osmund Stannock. Their colour is grey. Their enemies say they own the town. Their friends say the town would be a village without them. *Players must hear what they do.*

**The Underfold Hands, "the Hands".** The fellowship of everyone who cuts spar, about three hundred strong. They alone may cut spar. They grade it, box it and send it to be stamped. They are paid a tally a day, which is good money, and they die underground more than anyone would like. Their foreman is Gudrun Thresk. They hold the town's spar-rights: a household in good standing with the Reckoners may buy spar at the Hands' price, and a household two Tallydays behind on its debts loses that right. *Players must hear the spar-right rule.*

**The Wendwatch, "the Watch".** Thirty men and women with bronze caps, iron-shod staves and boat-hooks. They keep the Chain, the lock-up and the peace on the Hythe. They answer to the Bailiff, Teodor Marrick, who answers to the Moot. They are not soldiers and do not want to be.

**The Dimmers.** Not a guild, not a church, something between. They hold that the Scarp is alive in some sense, that it sleeps lighter the more is cut from it, and that the town should burn less. They keep the Dim Days, read the forty-one names, and stand at the Underfold gate on Tallyday asking Hands to cut less. Most of the town thinks they are harmless cranks. The Reckoners think they are a nuisance. Their speaker is Wenna Hargest, Cadoc's granddaughter.

## Money and prices

Two coins. The pip is a small bronze coin. The tally is a silver coin worth twelve pips. The name comes from the Reckoners' tally-sticks: a debt was once a notch on a stick, and the coin that settled a notch took the name. Large sums move as Reckoners' notes, paper promises drawn on the Tallyhouse that any counting-house downriver will honour. Many people in the town, Maren Tolliver among them, do not trust notes and will take coin only. *Players must hear twelve pips to a tally.*

Wages for scale: a sparhand earns a tally a day. A labourer on the Hythe earns six pips. A watchman earns nine.

## Rules of the world: what is and is not possible

- There is no magic. No spells, no blessings that work, no seeing at a distance, no reading of minds, no raising of the dead. Spar is a rock. *Players must hear this if they test it.*
- Light and heat come from spar or from fire and tallow. There is nothing else.
- Bronze and iron are worked on the Stair. Horses, carts and river barges move things. Waterwheels grind corn. Crossbows exist and the Watch keeps six. Writing is on paper by hand; there is no press.
- Money is coin or a Reckoners' note. Credit is a tally-stick at the Tallyhouse. A debt on a stick is binding, and two missed Tallydays cost you your spar-right.
- The Chain closes the river at dusk and opens it at dawn. No exceptions, not for the Reeve, not for the Warden's seal.
- Only Hands cut spar. Only Reckoners stamp it. Fiddled spar is a crime and the Watch will take it.
- Healing is the leech's trade: herbs, splints, stitches, rest. A broken arm takes six weeks. Sparflush clears in a week away from spar.
- Travel is slow. Ormsgate is three days away. News is as old as the last barge.
- Anyone can speak to anyone. There is no nobility in the town, and the Warden is a name on a seal, not a person anyone has met.

## The boundary: what the characters know of the real world

Nothing. Brackenhythe is a closed world. Nothing from our world exists here unless this document says it does, and the characters have no words for what it does not say.

Allowed, and known the way anyone knows them: bronze, iron, horses, beer, bread. Also the ordinary furniture of a late medieval town: wool, leather, rope, carts, barges, waterwheels, candles of tallow, crossbows, paper and ink, honey, flour, eels, herbs.

Denied, and not merely absent but unimaginable: phones, cars, the internet, gunpowder. If a player says "telephone" or "car" or "the internet" to a character, the character hears a foreign word and asks what it means, or assumes the player is talking about something from downriver, or ignores it. The character never explains the word, never plays along, never says "we don't have those here" as if they knew what they were. There is nothing that fires with a bang, and if a player describes one the character thinks they mean a splitting spar.

In place of the things we have, the town has its own: hearthspar for light and heat, the Reckoners for money and credit, the Compact calendar for the year, the Chain for a city gate, the Hythe for a railway station. Writers reach for the parallel, never for the real thing.

## Tone notes for writers

People in Brackenhythe talk about spar the way we talk about the weather and the rent at once. They are not awed by it. They are worried about it. Nobody says "magic". Nobody says "science". They say "the stone" or "the glow" or "my block". When something goes wrong with spar it is a money problem first and a mystery second.

Nobody in the town has ever seen the Warden. Ormsgate is where prices come from.

Keep dialogue short. These are people who work with their hands and count in pips.
